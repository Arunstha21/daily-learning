How to explain an idea clearly using Context, Point, and Example

Outcome: In about 12 minutes, you will be able to structure a short explanation so a listener can follow the idea and remember it.

About 12 minutes Level 1 26 July 2026
1

The problem: the listener starts lost

You know an idea well. When you explain it, you often begin with details, history, or technical terms. The other person has no frame for those details, so the main idea never lands.

Clear explanations usually give the listener three things in order: enough background to care, a plain statement of the idea, and one concrete picture of it in action.

The central idea: Structure a short explanation as Context → Point → Example. Context prepares the listener, the Point states the idea, and the Example makes it real and memorable.
2

One picture: giving directions

Imagine a visitor asks how to reach the post office.

  • Context: “You are standing at the main gate of the park.”
  • Point: “Walk straight for two blocks, then turn left.”
  • Example: “You will pass a red tea shop; the post office is the next building after it.”

Without the first sentence the visitor does not know where to start. Without the last sentence the route stays abstract. The same three moves work for almost any short explanation.

3

The three supporting moves

  1. Context — Give only the background the listener needs so the idea fits. Skip history that does not help the next sentence.
  2. Point — State the main idea in one clear sentence. Prefer ordinary words. Put the point early so the listener knows what to listen for.
  3. Example — Offer one concrete case, story, or comparison. Abstract ideas become memorable when the listener can picture them.
Common mix-up: Context is not a long lecture. It is the minimum frame that stops the listener asking “Why are you telling me this?” The Point is not the whole story; it is the single idea you want remembered.
4

Worked example: explaining DNS in 30 seconds

Suppose a colleague asks what DNS does. A common unclear answer starts with hierarchy, resolvers, and cache times. A Context-Point-Example version stays short:

  1. Context: “When you type a website name, your computer needs a numeric address to contact the server.”
  2. Point: “DNS is the system that looks up the address that belongs to that name.”
  3. Example: “It works like an address book: you look up ‘Maya’ and the book returns the phone number you actually dial.”

The listener now has a reason to care, a clear definition, and a picture that sticks. Extra technical detail can follow only if they ask.

5

Do not mix up these jobs

MoveMain jobWhat it is not
ContextGive the listener a reason and a starting placeA full history or every related fact
PointState the single idea you want rememberedA list of supporting details
ExampleMake the idea concrete and memorableAnother abstract restatement
6

Try it in under three minutes

Choose one idea you already know (a work process, a household tip, or the DNS idea from the previous lesson).

Write three short sentences:

  1. Context — one sentence of background.
  2. Point — the main idea in plain words.
  3. Example — one concrete case or comparison.

Then say the three sentences aloud. Stop after 30 seconds.

The goal is a usable structure, not perfect wording. You can refine it later.

Three-line recap

  1. Context prepares the listener so the idea has a place to land.
  2. The Point states the main idea early and in plain language.
  3. One concrete Example turns the idea into something the listener can picture and remember.

Communication practice

Complete this sentence in plain language for a non-technical listener:

“I can explain this idea clearly by first giving context, then stating the point, and finally adding an example, because…”

A good answer mentions that the listener needs a frame, a clear main idea, and a concrete picture.

Sources and fact check

  • Mensh B, Kording K. Ten simple rules for structuring papers. PLOS Computational Biology. Advocates the context-content-conclusion (C-C-C) scheme so readers are not left asking “Why was I told that?” or “So what?”; published 28 September 2017.
  • Grossman E. How to present complex ideas clearly. British Council Voices. Emphasises simple language, real-life examples, and analogies; published 4 May 2015 (still useful for the stable techniques).
  • PREP (Point–Reason–Example–Point) is a widely used speaking structure popularised by Brian Tracy in presentation training materials; it places the main idea early and reinforces it with a concrete example.
  • Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF) is a standard military communication practice that places the main point at the start of a message so the audience knows what matters immediately.

Sources checked 26 July 2026. Older foundational guidance is used because the structural advice remains stable; recent explanatory articles confirm the same practical pattern of context, clear statement, and concrete illustration.

Q&A: answer before you reveal

Without a brief frame the listener does not know why the idea matters or how to place it, so the Point can feel disconnected or confusing.
